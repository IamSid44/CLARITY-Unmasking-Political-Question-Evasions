\section{Conclusion and Plan to the Final Evaluation}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Our controlled encoder stops at 0.384 Subtask~2 macro-F1 per model (0.405 as a system), and the evidence says the limit is the model, not the labels or the decision rule.
Swapping only the backbone for a LoRA-tuned 8B decoder lifts the system to 0.543 and 0.746 on all ten paired seeds.
The gain is broad, which suggests that a larger pretrained model is simply better at reading what kind of response an answer is.

\paragraph{Uses beyond this task.}
For political interviews, the system flags how directly an answer addresses its question, which could help journalists and fact-checkers find non-answers in large archives.
The same design should suit three settings we have not tested.
In \emph{earnings calls}, an executive's evasive reply to an analyst can carry information investors care about.
In \emph{parliamentary hearings}, oversight depends on whether officials actually answer the questions put to them.
In \emph{chatbot evaluation}, the key check is whether a reply resolves the question that was asked.

\paragraph{Goal.}
Our single-pass system is 0.074 below the winning pipeline on Subtask~2 (0.543 against 0.617 on dev) but makes no LLM call at all, whereas that pipeline makes 1.94 calls with about 7,300 input and 380 output tokens per item \citep{shi-etal-2026-teleai}.
Yet an acceptable label is in Qwen's top three for 93.1\% of items (top five, 99.1\%), and its confidence separates easy items from hard ones (in-set rate 0.87 in the most confident fifth, 0.40 in the least, \S\ref{sec:analysis}), so an LLM should be needed on only some items.
We therefore plan a confidence-routed cascade of four modules, described below, drawn in Figure~\ref{fig:plan} and scheduled in Table~\ref{tab:timeline}, each added as one change under the protocol of \S\ref{sec:task}.
We target at least 0.60 dev Subtask~2 with at most 0.3 LLM calls per item, about a sixth of the winner's.

\paragraph{M1: calibrated candidate generator.}
M1 is the Qwen3-8B-Base classifier with LoRA, trained for the twelve epochs adopted on the train slice (\S\ref{sec:llm}).
Its $K$ seeds are averaged and logit-adjusted (Eq.~\ref{eq:la}) to give $\bar p(c \mid x)$, and since each seed costs one forward pass, $K$ is our first cost lever.
Instead of a single label, M1 returns a candidate set $C(x)$, the smallest set of top-ranked labels whose adjusted probability reaches a threshold.
We calibrate that threshold on cross-fitted training probabilities (5 folds, so no item is scored by a model trained on it) so that $C(x)$ contains the gold label for 95\% of held-out items.
M1 also returns an uncertainty score $u(x)$, a logistic regression fitted on the train slice to predict M1's errors from the top probability, the margin to the second label and seed disagreement.

\paragraph{M2: uncertainty router.}
M2 defers the fraction $\delta$ of items with the highest $u(x)$.
Its threshold is the $(1-\delta)$ quantile of $u$ on the train slice, so the deferral rate carries over to unseen data.
Items not deferred keep M1's answer, so LLM calls per item equal $\delta$.
Each $\delta \in \{0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, 1\}$ gives one point on an accuracy-vs-cost curve, and the operating point is fixed on the train slice.

\paragraph{M3: candidate-constrained LLM adjudicator.}
An open-weight instruction-tuned LLM, served locally with vLLM, reads the sub-question, the full question and the answer, and may only choose among the labels in $C(x)$.
Its prompt gives definitions of the candidate labels, a confusion guide with contrast rules for our three largest error pairs (\S\ref{sec:analysis}), and up to ten boundary examples, the training items nearest the answer in embedding space whose gold labels are in $C(x)$.
Without writing out any reasoning, the model scores the tokens of each candidate label, which gives a distribution $q(c \mid x)$ over $C(x)$ from a single forward pass with almost no output tokens.
M3 then combines the two models as $\lambda \log \bar p + (1-\lambda)\log q - \tau \log \pi$ over $C(x)$, with $\lambda$ and $\tau$ fitted by nested cross-validation and prompts designed on the train slice only.
These components carried the top systems (\S\ref{sec:related}), and on the winner's final stage definitions with a confusion guide raised Subtask~2 from 0.491 to 0.593 and ten boundary examples took it to 0.617 \citep{shi-etal-2026-teleai}.

\paragraph{M4: distillation and compression.}
We run M3 once on every training item, using cross-fitted candidates from M1, and mix its distribution $q$ with the gold label as a soft target for retraining M1 \citep{hinton2015distilling}, with the mixing weight fitted on the train slice.
A sharper M1 should need fewer deferrals for the same accuracy (distillation raised the winning team's 7B model from 0.587 to 0.758 on Subtask~1 but only from 0.495 to 0.509 on Subtask~2, \citealp{shi-etal-2026-teleai}).
Finally, M1 and M3 run with 4-bit weights, and each operating point reports the measured calls, tokens and GPU-seconds per item.
